Why Is My Laundry Room So Hot?

A dryer moves a surprising amount of heat and water. Every load carries pounds of moisture out of the clothes and sends it, warm and damp, into the duct. If your laundry room is hot and sticky while the dryer runs, that load of heat and water is not making it outside. The question is where it is stopping.

Where the Heat Is Supposed to Go

The dryer heats air, passes it through the drum to pick up moisture from the clothes, and pushes it out through the duct to a termination on a wall or roof. When that pathway works, the room stays close to ambient temperature because the heat leaves with the air. When the pathway is restricted or broken, the heat and the moisture have to go somewhere, and the nearest somewhere is the room you are standing in.

Four Reasons It Stays Inside

1. The duct is restricted

The commonest cause by a wide margin. Lint has narrowed the run to the point where the blower cannot push the full volume through it, so some of the hot exhaust backs up and leaks out at the joints, at the connection behind the machine, and around the lint filter housing. This is a clogged dryer vent, and the airflow reading at the outside hood will be visibly weak.

2. The duct has come apart

A joint that separated inside a wall or an attic. The exhaust leaves the duct freely — just into the building rather than outdoors. The tell here is different from a clog: there will be almost no air at the outside hood while the dryer behaves fairly normally, because the air is escaping without resistance. If you can get into the attic and there is a fine grey film spreading across the insulation, that is the confirmation. This needs reconnection, and it is worth dealing with sooner than a clog, because every load is putting moisture into the structure.

3. The connector behind the machine is crushed

The short flexible hose between the dryer and the wall gets folded every time the machine is pushed back. Fold it often enough and it stays folded. We find plenty of otherwise clean runs choked in the first eighteen inches, and the fix is a new transition hose cut to the shortest workable length — a small job that frequently makes an immediate difference to the room temperature.

4. The termination flap will not open

The hood outside has a flap that is supposed to swing open under exhaust pressure and fall shut afterwards. Painted shut, lint-jammed, warped, or fitted with a mesh pest screen that has clogged solid, it becomes a closed door at the end of an otherwise clear duct. That is repair work, and it is often the single thing that fixes the problem.

The Florida Complication

In a dry climate a hot laundry room is uncomfortable. In South Florida it is a humidity problem as well, because the air that is not leaving is carrying a load of water with it. An interior laundry room with no window and a restricted duct can hold that moisture for hours after the cycle finishes, and in a closed-up house with the air conditioning running, it ends up as a load on the whole system rather than just one room.

The Five-Minute Check

You can narrow this down yourself before calling anyone:

  • Run the dryer on a heated cycle and go outside to the termination hood.
  • Hold a hand near it. Strong, warm, steady air means the run is clear and something else is going on. A weak trickle means a restriction. Nothing at all, with the dryer clearly running, points at a separation.
  • Watch the flap. If it never lifts, that is your answer regardless of what is behind it.
  • Pull the dryer forward and look at the hose. Ribbed silver foil, white plastic, or anything folded over on itself is worth replacing.
  • Feel around the lint filter housing while the dryer runs. Hot air pushing back out there means the exhaust cannot get away.

When It Is Not the Vent

Sometimes the room is hot for reasons that have nothing to do with the duct. A laundry room in an unconditioned garage will be hot in July whatever the dryer is doing. A closed interior closet with no return air will heat up simply because a heat-producing appliance is running in a sealed box. And a dryer with a failing thermostat can run hotter than it should with a perfectly clear vent, which is an appliance problem rather than ours.

The airflow check above is what separates these. If air at the outside hood is strong and steady and the room is still hot, the duct is not your problem and an appliance technician is a better call than a vent company.

A hot laundry room is a symptom, not a diagnosis. The useful thing is knowing whether the exhaust is leaving the building, and that takes about five minutes to establish.

What We Do About It

We trace the run, take an airflow reading before touching anything, and tell you which of the four causes you are looking at. If it is lint, a cleaning handles it. If the duct is damaged or disconnected, cleaning will not help and we say so rather than doing it anyway. Either way you get the before-and-after numbers, which is the part that is hard to argue with.
