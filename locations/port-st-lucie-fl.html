What Fast Growth Leaves Behind

A New House Is Not the Same as a Clean Duct System

Port St. Lucie has grown faster than almost anywhere in the state, and most of that growth arrived as single-family houses on slab — one or two stories, air handler in the garage or an interior closet, envelope sealed up tight. Tight is good for cooling. It is less good for whatever found its way into the ductwork before you had keys, because there is nowhere for it to go.

The finishing trades are the usual source. Compound gets sanded, flooring gets cut, and the rough-in trunk lines sit open through all of it. Years later that residue is still in the boots under the registers and along the first few feet of flex. Cleaning the accessible components will not make a used system new, but it removes what can actually be reached — and we photograph before and after so the difference is yours to judge, not ours to describe.

Floor Plans & Vent Routing

Where These Builders Put the Laundry Room

The second pattern worth knowing here is placement. In a great many of these plans the laundry sits off a hallway or between the garage and the kitchen — interior, not against an outside wall. So the duct climbs into the attic, turns two or three times across the trusses, and terminates at a roof cap or a gable-end hood. Every turn is a place lint slows down and settles.

Long runs also make a dryer work harder for the same load. If cycle times have crept up, if the laundry room feels damp when a load finishes, or if the cap outside barely puffs while the machine is running, airflow is telling you something. The answer is not always a cleaning — sometimes it is a crushed foil transition behind the machine, or a fine pest screen doing exactly what screens do. We will tell you which one you have.

Asked by Port St. Lucie Homeowners

Questions About Newer Construction

My Port St. Lucie home is only a few years old — do the ducts really need cleaning? +

New construction is not the same as clean construction. Drywall dust, sawdust and insulation fibers get pulled into open trunk lines while a house is being finished, and plenty of it is still sitting in the boots on move-in day. We clean the accessible supplies, returns and boots and photograph before and after, so you can judge the result rather than take our word for it.

Our laundry room sits in the middle of the floor plan. Does that make the dryer vent harder to clean? +

Mostly it makes it longer. When the laundry is not on an exterior wall, the duct climbs into the attic and turns two or three times before it reaches a cap. Dryer manufacturers publish a maximum equivalent duct length and every elbow counts against it, so an interior run with several turns can hold lint that a short exterior run never would. We work the run from both ends and tell you plainly if a section is not accessible.

Our dryer vent exits through the roof instead of a side wall. Can you still service it? +

Usually, yes. Roof terminations are common on newer single-story homes here. We clean the run from the laundry side and check the cap where it can be reached safely. Screened caps deserve a look either way, because screening catches lint quickly and is a common reason a load starts taking two cycles. If a cap cannot be reached safely, we say so instead of guessing.

Houses are still going up on our street. Should we wait until nearby construction finishes? +

If your own home is finished and lived in, there is no reason to wait. Site dust from neighboring lots mostly lands on your filter and around the registers rather than deep inside a closed duct system, so a fresh filter handles most of it. What is worth waiting on is work inside your own house — if drywall or flooring is planned, clean afterward, not before.
