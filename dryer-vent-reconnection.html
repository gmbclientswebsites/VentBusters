Dryer Vent Reconnection

A disconnected dryer duct does not just stop venting outside. It starts venting into your attic or your wall. It is one of the few vent faults that gets worse the longer it goes unnoticed, and most homeowners find it by accident.

Why This One Matters More

The Exhaust Has to Go Somewhere

A dryer moves a surprising amount of water. Every load carries pounds of moisture out of the clothes, and all of it leaves as warm vapour in the exhaust. When the duct is intact, that vapour goes outdoors. When a joint has let go in the attic, it goes into the attic.

In a humid climate that is a problem of a different kind from a clog. Warm damp air in an enclosed space raises the humidity in the structure, and the lint that comes with it accumulates on joists and insulation. A clog costs you drying time; a disconnection is depositing moisture and lint where neither belongs.

It happens because of how the joints are made. A great many residential runs rely on friction fit plus foil tape, and tape in an attic that reaches high temperatures every summer eventually dries out and releases. Nothing dramatic happens at the moment it lets go, which is why these are usually found months later.

What Happens
Exhaust vents into attic or wall
Usual Cause
Tape failure or friction-fit joints
Found By
Airflow reading plus attic inspection
Urgency
Higher than a normal clog
How You Find Out

Signs the Duct Has Come Apart

The pattern is different from a clog: airflow at the hood drops to almost nothing, but the dryer is not necessarily struggling.

💨

No air at the outside hood at all

Not weak — nothing. If the flap never moves while the dryer runs, the exhaust is leaving the duct before it gets there.

🧺

Lint on attic insulation or joists

A fine grey film spreading out from the duct route. It is the clearest confirmation there is.

🌬️

Humidity or a damp smell upstairs

Especially in a closed second floor or a hallway below the attic hatch.

💧

Ceiling stains along the duct route

Condensation inside the structure finding its way down. Worth acting on quickly.

🌡️

The attic feels warmer than it should

A dryer running into an attic is a space heater with a humidifier attached.

🐌

More insects than usual near the laundry

Warm, damp, lint-lined cavities are attractive. It is an indirect sign, but we have traced it back more than once.

⚠️ If you can see lint spreading across attic insulation, stop using the dryer until the duct has been looked at. Each load adds more moisture into the structure.
How We Fix It

Locate the Break, Then Make the Joint Properly

Reconnecting is quick. Finding the separation, and making sure it is the only one, is the part that takes care.

1

Confirm the symptom

An airflow reading at the termination with the dryer running. Near-zero airflow with a dryer that seems to be working normally points strongly at a separation rather than a clog.

2

Find the break

We follow the duct route from both ends as far as access allows, checking the attic where it is safe to enter. Lint deposits usually lead straight to the open joint.

3

Reconnect and secure

The joint is reseated to the proper overlap, fastened mechanically rather than with tape alone, and sealed. Where the run needs support so it cannot pull apart again, we add it.

4

Check the whole run

One failed joint often means others of the same age are close behind. We check what we can reach and tell you what we found, then re-measure airflow to confirm the exhaust is going outside again.

Scope

What a Reconnection Covers

What we can reconnect depends entirely on what we can reach, and we establish that before quoting.

  • Airflow reading to confirm a separation rather than a clog
  • Tracing the duct route from both ends, including safe attic access
  • Reseating the joint to a proper overlap and fastening it mechanically
  • Sealing the connection and supporting the run against recurrence
  • Checking remaining reachable joints on the same run
  • Airflow reading after the work, with photos of the repair

What this visit does not cover

  • No opening finished surfaces. A separation inside a closed wall cannot be reached without cutting, and that is a contractor’s work.
  • No attic entry where it is unsafe. Low clearance, no walkway, damaged decking, or extreme heat all mean we work from the ends instead.
  • No insulation replacement. We will show you lint-contaminated insulation; replacing it is an insulation contractor’s job.
  • No moisture remediation. We do not assess or treat anything growing in a damp attic, and we will not pretend otherwise.

What changes the price

How hard the break is to find and reach. A separation a few feet into an accessible attic with a walkway is a short job. One in the middle of a low-clearance attic over a second floor is not.

If the duct is also damaged where it came apart, or if the run turns out to be foil that will separate again, we will quote repair or replacement for that portion instead of reconnecting something that will not hold.

Questions

Dryer Vent Reconnection FAQ

How can I tell a disconnection from a clog? +

Airflow at the outside hood is the tell. A clog usually leaves some air moving and a dryer that is clearly struggling. A separation often leaves no air at the hood at all while the dryer behaves fairly normally, because the exhaust is escaping freely — just in the wrong place.

Is a disconnected dryer vent dangerous? +

It is a problem worth dealing with promptly. You are putting heat, moisture and lint into an enclosed part of your house with every load, and lint accumulating around a warm duct is not something to leave indefinitely. We would not use words like emergency, but we would not leave it for a few months either.

Can you reconnect duct inside a wall? +

Only if there is an access point we can reach it through. If the separation is in a closed wall cavity, reaching it means opening the wall, which is a contractor’s job rather than ours. We will tell you where we believe the break is and what reaching it would involve.

Will you tell me if there is mould in my attic? +

We will tell you what we can see, and we will show you photos. What we will not do is assess it, identify it, or treat it — that is a different licensed discipline and not something we offer. If an attic looks like it needs that kind of assessment, we will say so and leave it to the people qualified to do it.

How do I stop it happening again? +

Mechanical fastening instead of tape alone, and proper support so the run is not hanging on its joints. Those two changes are most of the answer. If the duct is flexible foil, the honest answer is that it will keep doing this, and replacing that section is the real fix.

No Air at All From the Outside Vent?

That pattern usually means the duct has come apart rather than clogged. Tell us what you are seeing and we will tell you where to look.
